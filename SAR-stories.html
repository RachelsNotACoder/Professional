Situation

During a migration of the core Rent Payments retargeting flow from Klaviyo to Customer.io, I recognized the existing flow was several years old and built on a fear/loss-aversion messaging model tied to a landlord persona that no longer matched our broader acquisition mix.

Action

I hypothesized that landlords didn't recognize themselves in the old messaging. They viewed rent collection as a solved problem, even if their actual "solution" was manual and tedious. Version B dropped the fear framing entirely and focused on removing drudgery: manual reminders, chasing late payments, writing receipts. I emphasized that the feature was free and saved hours of admin work. I built an A/B path test within the migration itself to validate the hypothesis.

Result

The automation-framed path delivered a 72% lift in Rent Payment adoption. Rent Payments is a primary ARR driver via tenant fees and the platform's stickiest retention feature, creating a recurring monthly engagement loop with landlords.

Situation

TurboTenant's onboarding series for incomplete signups hadn't been updated in some time, despite meaningful changes in the acquisition mix. For two specific intent segments, the series wasn't beating holdout: the emails were producing no measurable lift over doing nothing.

Action

I hypothesized that a shift in the acquisition mix on the landlord portfolio side was driving the gap: all messaging had been written for small landlords, systematically ignoring larger landlords, who behaved differently—lower open rates but significantly higher conversion when they did open, and entirely different value propositions. I rebuilt the series as a single journey per intent segment, using Liquid scripting to serve differentiated content blocks by property count. For larger landlords, I surfaced the Payments Dashboard and optimized subject lines to lift open rates. For smaller landlords, messaging stayed benefit-forward at their scale. A/B tested old vs. new.

Result

+11% relative lift in property-add completion for Marketing intent signups and +5% lift for Rent Payment intent signups. Both cleared holdout performance the original series had never reached. Durable learning: larger landlords convert at a disproportionate rate when they open.

Situation

TurboTenant's incomplete-signup onboarding series had multiple intent segments, both failing to beat holdout. The core problem: all messaging had been written for small landlords. Larger landlords existed in both segments but responded to completely different value propositions. Before I could build anything, I had a structural decision to make: split journeys by portfolio size, use Liquid inside a single template, or build separate journeys with different entry criteria. Each has different implications for maintainability, scalability, and, critically, what you can measure afterward.

Action

Chose Liquid inside a single journey per intent segment. The structure of the experience was identical for both landlord sizes: same sequence, same timing, same channel. What varied was content. Portfolio size is a continuous variable, not a clean binary: a hard split line would mean an arbitrary bucketing decision and two template sets to maintain indefinitely. Liquid let me treat it as a spectrum: one template, conditional content blocks rendering based on property count. But Liquid creates a measurement problem journey splits don't: the platform records one send, one open, one click. It doesn't tell you which content block rendered for which user. So I instrumented it deliberately, passing a dynamic UTM parameter (utm_content set to landlord size segment) on every CTA link. That gave me a clean downstream read: not just "did the new series beat the old one" but "which segment drove the lift."

Result

+11% lift for Marketing intent signups, +5% for Rent Payment intent. Both cleared holdout. Key learning confirmed by the instrumentation: larger landlords had lower open rates but dramatically higher conversion when they did open. One maintainable template per segment, with measurement built in from the start rather than retrofitted.

Situation

A fintech startup gave me CSV files covering their funnel metrics and email performance and asked me to diagnose their primary conversion problem. They were looking for a lifecycle or messaging fix.

Action

Used AI end-to-end to interpret the data, structure the diagnosis, and draft the output deck. The data surfaced an immediate discrepancy: there was a significant conversion gap at the final purchase step, but the platform breakdown told the real story. Web was converting at 45%, iOS at 60%, but Android was at 30%. That's not a messaging problem. That's a product problem. I flagged it as a likely Android SDK issue rather than a lifecycle or copy fix, which reframed the entire prioritization. Also identified a secondary issue: their email program was structured like acquisition landing pages—long scrolls, broad value props—which doesn't match inbox behavior. Presented findings via deck and Loom, then live Q&A.

Result

The Android finding redirected diagnostic attention from marketing to engineering, the right call when half your mobile conversions are leaking at the final step. When leadership pushed back on the email recommendations, I held the position with evidence rather than deferring: inbox behavior doesn't support long-scroll content consumption, and the baseline expectation should be set correctly before testing alternatives.

Situation

At Wells Fargo, working in yield optimization on unsecured revolving credit, CX was fielding a steady stream of complaints about interest rates—customers comparing them to mortgages and home equity loans and feeling gouged. The CX team's proposed solution was to give reps more latitude to lower rates.

Action

Our rates were well-priced for unsecured revolving credit, and a blanket rate-concession policy would have been a revenue and margin problem. But "let me explain how unsecured credit risk works" doesn't work at a service desk. I needed something that protected rate integrity without leaving CX without a move to make. The real issue was that customers were comparing the wrong products: if someone was upset about a revolving credit rate and they owned a home, they were likely a candidate for a home equity product, which genuinely carries a lower rate for legitimate structural reasons. I reframed the complaint as a qualification signal, and wrote a script that gave reps a clean redirect: acknowledge the frustration, then pivot to "would it help if I explored whether a home equity option might be a better fit?" Worked with supervisors to get it in front of the team.

Result

Rather than saying no and leaving CX without a path forward, the script gave representatives a way to turn what had felt like a thankless de-escalation into a genuine earning opportunity—acknowledge the concern, demonstrate empathy, and potentially earn commission on a home equity cross-sell in the same conversation. The more durable result was relational: the approach showed the support team that I cared about customers as much as they did while also understanding what their day actually looked like. That combination of commercial alignment and empathy for their role built the kind of partnership that made future collaboration easier.

Situation

Situation

Situation

When I joined TurboTenant as the first dedicated lifecycle hire, I inherited a campaign environment built by multiple teams over time: Product Management, Product Marketing, and lifecycle, each scoped around individual features. No one was looking at what any single user received across all of them at once. Users were complaining about getting too many messages, but "too many messages" wasn't specific enough to act on. I needed to know exactly which users were hit hardest and which campaigns were causing it.

Action

I audited the existing campaigns and send logs across two dimensions: which users were receiving the highest message volume, and which campaigns showed up most often in the collisions. That gave me a concrete picture of where the problem was worst, instead of a general sense that volume was too high. From there I built a prioritization and frequency cap system directly in Customer.io. High-priority campaigns, the ones where a timely send mattered to the user experience, got an exemption flag so they'd always send regardless of message count. Everything else fell under a global rule capping how many messages a user could receive in a rolling window. I implemented it and told stakeholders after the fact instead of asking for approval first. I had the send log data to back up the decision, and getting something concrete in front of people would move faster than debating a proposal. A few senior stakeholders came back asking which of their campaigns should be exempted. That was the right response: they weren't fighting the system, they were working inside it.

Result

The number of users hitting the frequency ceiling dropped sharply once the system went in, confirming the audit had found the right collision points. Unsubscribe rate declined in the period that followed, though other variables were in play too, so I won't claim full credit for that number. The bigger, more durable change was structural: the program went from uncoordinated to governed, with a framework that holds as new campaigns get added.

Situation

TurboTenant's Refer-a-Friend targeting logic lived in-product, feeding the old platform we were migrating away from. It triggered the referral ask three months after signup, gated only by "revenue greater than zero," which counted any paid feature usage, including features the landlord hadn't actually bought into. It re-asked every 90 days for anyone active on the platform at least once in that window. Because the trigger lived in-product rather than in marketing tooling, Marketing couldn't adjust or test it without engineering time we didn't have.

Action

As part of the platform migration, I rebuilt the trigger from the ground up with an eye toward tighter targeting and Marketing ownership. Instead of a three-month signup clock, the ask fires three days after a user's first revenue event, catching people right after they experience real value instead of on an arbitrary calendar. For the recurring ask, instead of "active at least once in 90 days" (a weak signal that counted things like checking old records or editing a profile), the new check required revenue to have increased since the last check-in—a real signal someone was still getting ongoing value, not just still logged in. The whole trigger moved out of product and into the marketing platform, so it would never again be something only engineering could touch.

Result

Referral sends per 1,000 moved from roughly 10 to 15 overall, and for newly onboarded customers specifically, from roughly 25 to 39 per 1,000, a bigger relative jump for the segment already most likely to refer. Sends to established, technically-active-but-not-growing accounts dropped slightly, the right tradeoff given how much stronger the new-customer response rate already was. I don't have a clean, isolated lift number I'd stand behind in a room—the platform migration and the seasonality of the landlord business were both moving at the same time—but the targeting itself got measurably tighter, and Marketing owned a lever it had never directly controlled before.

Situation
