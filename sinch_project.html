Take-home case study · Sinch (Mailgun)

From signup to paid: three interventions that change the Mailgun activation curve

Senior Lifecycle Marketing Manager take-home assignment. Journey mapping, intervention design, and a cross-channel extension for one moment in the funnel.

Diagnosis Journey map Interventions Cross-channel

Diagnosis

Two problems, and a third one downstream

Activation friction. A significant share of signups never complete product activation (defined as sending an email from production). In a developer-facing product, that's a setup problem, and the setup step every self-serve user has to clear before sending anything real is domain and DNS configuration.

Shallow usage. Users who do activate then plateau: sending, but never touching suppression management, templates, inbound routing, or validation. Depth of usage, not just activation, is what the data says predicts upgrade.

A third moment sits downstream of both: once a user is activated and using the product with some depth, there's a window, approaching the free-plan sending ceiling, where the job is timing, not persuasion.

Scoped out deliberately

Two early steps aren't included below. Account verification (email and phone) is close to a solved problem; anyone who won't complete it is low-intent by definition, and there's no data here suggesting a real drop-off worth designing around. Signup itself is too early to act on.

Part 1

Intervention design

Intervention 1 · Waiting on domain verification

Three branches, each with a different job

A user has submitted a custom domain for verification. From there, three different things can happen: it can stall (DNS still propagating, no error, still within the expected window), it can get rejected (the domain provider returns a verification-rejected response, sometimes within minutes), or the user can abandon the flow before it resolves (no error, no completion, no in-product activity for 24+ hours).

Every branch also splits on a second axis: is the user in-product with cookies enabled, or not. That split matters as much as the first, since roughly 40–50% of users are invisible to the in-app tool at any given moment, regardless of which branch they're in.

Branch A — Stall

Trigger: domain submitted, not yet verified, no error, elapsed under 48 hours.

In-product, cookies enabled

Banner

Ambient and non-blocking. DNS propagation is expected, nothing's broken, so the surface should update the user without demanding a response. A tooltip dismisses on hover-away and a modal interrupts; neither fits a wait that's supposed to resolve on its own.

Message — banner, hasn't tested in sandbox yet

"DNS can take up to 48 hours to propagate. While you wait, try a sandbox send."

CTA: Test in sandbox
Message — banner, already tested in sandbox

"DNS can take up to 48 hours to propagate. You've already tested in sandbox, so get a head start on production."

CTA: Set up production send

Not in-product, or no cookies, inactive 10+ minutes

Email

The only surface that reaches someone outside the tab. Delayed to 10 minutes so it doesn't fire redundantly at someone still actively watching the in-app banner.

Email — stall delay

Subject: Your domain verification is still in progress

"DNS can take up to 48 hours to propagate, so [domain] may not be verified yet. If you haven't already, you can test your integration in the sandbox while you wait. We'll email you the moment verification completes."

CTA: Test in sandbox

Hyperlinks: → sandbox domain setup, in-product→ production domain setup, in-product

Success criteria: % who take a sandbox or production-setup action during the wait, median time to first production send, % of verifications completing while the user is still in-product.

Branch B — Rejected

Trigger: domain provider returns a verification-rejected response (bad record, mismatch, conflict).

In-product, cookies enabled

Banner, alert treatment

Same Gainsight Bar component as the stall banner, styled to read as urgent: color, icon, persistent placement until resolved. A real problem exists now, so it needs more visual weight than the ambient stall version. It still isn't modal-worthy. The user can keep working elsewhere while they decide how to fix it.

Message — banner, alert treatment

"We couldn't verify [domain]: [reason]."

CTA: Fix it · Invite a teammate

Not in-product, or no cookies

Email

Same reasoning as the stall email: if they're not there to see the in-app alert, email has to carry the same specifics on its own, with no in-app follow-up to lean on.

Email — verification rejected

Subject: Action needed: we couldn't verify [domain]

"We weren't able to verify [domain]. Here's why: [reason]. If DNS isn't something you handle yourself, loop in the teammate who manages it. Or reach out and we'll walk you through the fix."

CTA: See how to fix this · Invite a teammate · Talk to support

Personalization opportunity

The rejected reason often correlates with the domain provider (GoDaddy, Cloudflare, Namecheap, Google Domains, and so on), so both the in-app message and the DNS-records screen could be tailored per provider instead of generic. Where provider isn't captured directly, time-to-rejection is a cheaper proxy: a rejection that surfaces within seconds of submission is usually a simple syntax issue, while one that only shows up after normal propagation time has passed suggests a deeper conflict, like an existing conflicting record, that's less self-serve-friendly and might warrant leaning harder on the support and teammate-invite paths instead of the fix-it-yourself one.

Hyperlinks: → DNS records screen, showing the expected values→ direct line to support→ teammate invite flow

Success criteria: % resolved by the original user vs. via teammate invite vs. via support vs. unresolved after 7 days, teammate invite acceptance rate, support contact rate on this specific error state.

Branch C — Abandon

Trigger: domain submitted, no error, no completion, no in-product session in 24+ hours.

Email

In-app can't reach someone who isn't in the product, so this branch is email by definition. The copy is written to be fully self-contained, since there's no in-app surface to fall back on.

Email — verification abandoned

Subject: Your Mailgun domain verification is still waiting on you

"Verification for [domain] hasn't completed, and we haven't seen any activity on your account in a while. If DNS setup isn't something you handle yourself, invite a teammate who manages your domain so it doesn't stall your launch."

CTA: Invite a teammate · See setup instructions

Hyperlinks: → teammate invite flow→ domain setup, in-product

Success criteria: email open and click rate, % of abandoned verifications that resume within 7 days, % using the invite-teammate link specifically.

The commercial thread

When a teammate accepts an invite through the rejected or abandon branch, that's a second person actively collaborating on setup, which is itself paid-tier behavior (multi-user access sits on paid plans). A 30-day trial of the multi-user plan, triggered automatically on invite acceptance, turns a friction-relief mechanism into a real commercial entry point. This carries into Part 2.

The same invite-a-teammate idea belongs at stage 3 (sending in production) too, for users without engineering support, worded as "need a hand with the code? Loop in a developer" rather than the DNS-specific version above.

Intervention 2 · Missing capabilities beyond core sending

A discovery nudge, split by persona

A user has activated and is sending reliably, but hasn't touched suppression lists, templates, inbound routing, or validation. Splits into two inferred personas: developer (API-heavy, little dashboard use) and marketer/growth (dashboard-heavy, less code). The DNS and nameserver setup captured back at domain verification adds a second signal: some will skew developer, some non-technical product or marketing, some solopreneur. I would also check with Growth to see if anything is captured with source during signup; certain campaigns likely skew as well; if that information rolls up to UTMs or other acquisition information.

Trigger: 14+ consecutive days of sending, zero use of a second capability category, cross-referenced against inferred persona signals (dashboard vs. API usage, plus the nameserver signal from domain setup).

Slider

Not a Tooltip: too transient to hold a persona-specific message and a CTA before it's read. Not a Dialog: this is a discovery nudge, not an urgent one, and interrupting a working session to suggest a feature undercuts it.

Message — developer persona

"You're sending reliably. Most teams at your volume also use suppression lists to automatically keep bounces and complaints out of future sends. It's a two-minute setup, and it protects your sender reputation."

CTA: Set it up
Message — marketer/growth persona

"Your delivery rates are strong. Want to see what a templated, on-brand send looks like? Templates take the guesswork out of formatting and give you reusable, trackable campaigns."

CTA: See templates

Hyperlinks: → suppression list setup→ template gallery

Success criteria: % of exposed users who adopt a second capability within 30 days, correlation between second-capability adoption and upgrade at 60/90 days, click-through and dismissal rate by persona.

A/B test opportunity

Control: every eligible user sees one generic nudge, regardless of persona. Variant: the persona-branched version above. The metric that matters is upgrade rate at 60/90 days, not click-through or second-capability adoption alone, since a branched message could win engagement without moving revenue. Restricting the test to users the persona classifier is confident about keeps misclassified accounts from diluting the read.

Intervention 3 · Approaching the free plan ceiling

Two rungs, paced to the billing month rather than a rolling week

A user's sending volume is trending toward the free plan's cap. The clearest signal in the funnel: usage itself is telling the user they need more than the free plan gives them. The job is timing, catching this before the cap hits so the first message about upgrading isn't delivered alongside a service disruption.

Trigger: the cap resets on the calendar month, not a rolling 30 days, so a flat 7-day rolling average projected forward doesn't line up with a fixed month-end boundary. The projection instead blends two numbers: the account's month-to-date daily average (sends so far this month ÷ days elapsed), weighted toward a trailing 3-day average so a recent spike, a new campaign, say, gets caught faster than the flat average alone would. Projected month-end total = blended daily pace × days remaining in the month. The banner fires once that projection crosses the cap with 5 or fewer days left; the modal fires at 24 hours out if nothing's been done.

Requires: a daily job that computes the projection and writes it back as an account attribute, something like projected_cap_date, synced to both Gainsight, to drive the banner and modal below, and Customer.io, to drive the paired email. This is a data and analytics build before it's a messaging config.

Projected to hit the cap within 5 days, in-product, cookies enabled

Banner

Elevated but still non-blocking. There's real time left to act, so the surface should be visible and specific without stopping the user's work.

Message — banner (5-day threshold)

"You're on track to hit your free plan limit around [date]."

CTA: Upgrade now

Same threshold, not in-product or no cookies

Email

Same coverage-gap logic as domain verification: a user the in-app tool can't reach still needs the warning, so the same stored projection that drives the banner also drives a paired email through Customer.io.

Email — 5-day threshold

Subject: You're about to hit your Mailgun sending limit

"Your Mailgun account is on track to hit its free plan limit around [date], based on your current sending pace. Upgrading now keeps your sends running without interruption, no code changes required."

CTA: Upgrade now

Within 24 hours of the cap, no action taken

Modal

The one place in this case study a modal earns its interruption: imminent service disruption, and only after the banner and email already had their chance. Escalating straight to a modal at the first sign of the trend would be the wrong call. Earning it through the ladder is the point.

Message — modal (24-hour threshold)

"You're about to reach your sending limit."

CTA: Upgrade now

Hyperlinks: → plan upgrade flow, pre-filled with the recommended tier

Success criteria: % of at-risk accounts that upgrade before hitting the cap vs. after, reduction in cap-related support tickets, time from first exposure to upgrade decision, whether the banner-and-email pairing reduces how often accounts reach the modal stage over time.

Part 2

Cross-channel extension for domain verification

This is the moment worth extending. It's a high-volume moment, every self-serve user has to pass through it, so friction here has funnel-wide reach. It splits cleanly into three branches with different channel needs. And the teammate-invite mechanism is a genuine product change, not just a messaging layer, which is what this section is asking for.

The strategy

Keep users occupied in-product through an ordinary stall, but for the branches where in-app can't reach the user, a rejection they never saw or an outright abandon, route through email, and turn the resolution path itself, inviting a teammate, into a trial of the plan that includes multi-user access.

Who I'd need, and what I'd ask of them

Product, UX, engineering

The domain-status element on the product's own UI, the rejected-state banner treatment, and the invite-a-teammate flow. The status element is a one-time build that can ship independently, and it's the piece that reaches every user regardless of cookie consent, since it lives in the product itself, not the messaging tool.

Product (data / analytics)

Confirmation that domain_added and dns_propagated already reach both Gainsight and Customer.io with the fields and timing this needs, since those events almost certainly exist as core product telemetry already. The open question is whether a rejected response can be captured as its own distinct event rather than folded into a generic "not yet verified" state.

Engineering

Missing event routing, plus the trial-activation logic (invite accepted grants a 30-day multi-user trial), which touches billing and entitlements, not just messaging.

Growth / product, pricing & packaging

Sign-off on the trial mechanic itself. Auto-granting a paid-tier trial from an invite action is a pricing decision, not just a UX one.

Lifecycle / marketing ops

Banner and alert logic, UTM suppression rule.

Email platform owner

Three distinct triggers (stall-delay, rejected, abandon), each with different copy and CTA, plus trial-activation confirmation emails.

Support

A direct line, not just a help article, for the rejected branch. When verification fails, the user should be able to reach a person, not only a doc.

Data (success metrics owner)

Baseline and ongoing tracking split by branch: time to verification, % completing in-product vs. after leaving, ticket volume by branch, email click-through, teammate invite acceptance rate, trial-to-paid conversion from this entry point.

Handling the 40–50% coverage gap

This isn't a footnote here, it's the design constraint that shaped every branch above.

  • The domain-status element is the one piece every user sees, since it's product UI, not a cookie-gated messaging surface.
  • Email carries the rejected and abandon branches by necessity, not by cookie status: a user who hit a rejection they never saw, or who's already left, isn't reachable in-app regardless of consent. Both emails are written to be self-contained.
  • Cookie-enabled, still-active users get redundancy, not repetition: in-app for immediacy, the status element for reliability, email only if they've actually stepped away, with UTM suppression to prevent getting both.

Assumptions I'm carrying into this

  • That domain_added and dns_propagated are already instrumented (near-certain, since the product depends on them) and reach both the in-app tool and email today. Less certain: whether a rejected verification is captured as its own distinct event rather than lumped into "not yet verified."
  • Whether a rejected response can be classified with a specific reason programmatically, rather than a generic failure state.
  • Whether legal or finance would sign off on auto-granting a paid-tier trial from an invite action. That's a policy question, worth surfacing early.
  • What proportion of users are sandbox-first vs. going straight to production, which changes how much weight this intervention should carry relative to the others.
  • What share of the uncounted 50–60% is explicit opt-out vs. never having seen a consent prompt, and whether that varies geographically.
  • Current baseline numbers for time-to-verification and ticket volume by branch, needed in the first 30 days to know whether this is working.
